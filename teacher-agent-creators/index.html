THE QUESTION

The question is no longer whether teachers use AI.

When AI can act, assess, guide and generate—

how do teachers remain the pedagogical decision-makers?

The central challenge is not access to AI. It is pedagogical authority: teachers need to decide how AI participates in learning. [Sources] PDF p. 1; supporting page, opening section.

OUR ANSWER

Mature teacher AI competence is not using AI more often.

It is configuring when AI should act, when it should be constrained, when control should be reclaimed, and when AI should remain absent.

ACTCONSTRAINRECLAIMABSENT
We call this Boundary Learning. It shifts professional development from tool operation to responsible configuration of AI participation. Boundary Learning remains an ongoing synthesis, not a fully validated causal theory. [Sources] PDF pp. 27-29; supporting page, Boundary Learning framework.

01

FINDING · LEARNING BY MAKING

Creating agents externalizes tacit pedagogy.

Teachers turn an implicit teaching judgment into a runnable artifact—then test, revise and defend it.

DESIGN→TEST→DIAGNOSE→RECONFIGURE
The constructionism/ICAP/TPACK study argues that pedagogical agent creation can function as learning-by-making. The implication is to organize teacher development around creation, testing and reflection—not only lectures and prompt demonstrations. [Sources] PDF pp. 20, 22; AERA 2026 poster citation on PDF p. 25.

THE FRONTIER

Boundary Learning has four connected capacities.

01

Recognize the task boundary

Which tasks need AI—and which require struggle, dialogue or human judgment?

02

Diagnose the model boundary

Is failure caused by prompting, workflow, model limits or task mismatch?

03

Demarcate responsibility

Which decisions remain with teachers, students or peers?

04

Configure the boundary

Translate judgment into roles, constraints, timing and non-intervention zones.

The framework synthesizes four capacities: task boundary recognition, model boundary diagnosis, pedagogical responsibility demarcation and structured configuration. It is a working framework emerging from cross-cohort synthesis. [Sources] PDF pp. 27-29.

DESIGN ETHIC

Learning needs productive friction.

AI gives complete answers too earlyDelay feedback; require an attempt first
AI-generated scripts replace student voiceRequire peer critique before AI revision
AI rubric becomes rigidReturn criteria and final judgment to teachers
AI intervention ends discussionConstrain or disable AI to restore dialogue

The goal is not a frictionless classroom. It is the right friction, in the right place.

Boundary decisions become concrete when teachers delay, constrain, reclaim or remove AI participation. This protects productive cognitive challenge rather than optimizing for smooth automation. [Sources] PDF pp. 29-30.

WHAT EDUCATION SYSTEMS CAN DO

Move teacher AI policy from adoption to authorship.

  1. Design professional learning around creation.Teachers build, test, diagnose and revise—not just watch demonstrations.
  2. Assess artifacts and traces.Use workflows, agent architectures and revision histories alongside self-report.
  3. Embed support in schools.Protect time, autonomy, competence, peer community and follow-up.
  4. Make non-use a legitimate decision.Human oversight includes the authority to delay, constrain or exclude AI.
